⏺ Your README says one thing and the RFS says another, and that gap is your
  product problem:

  ▎ "Point us at the app you vibe-coded — we turn it into a real, live 
  ▎ product... As easy as posting a story."

  That's builder → live URL. The RFS thesis is builder → colleagues. Posting a
  story is broadcast; a Google Doc is shared with named people who have
  permissions. Everything below follows from picking the second.

  ---
  1. Who's the customer

  Three distinct roles, and conflating them is why this feels muddy:

  │           │ Their 5–30 colleagues who   │ Does it load, is my data      │
  │ Recipient │ use the app                 │ safe, do I have to make an    │
  │           │                             │ account                       │
  ├───────────┼─────────────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────┤
  │           │                             │ Is this sanctioned, secure,   │
  │ Customer  │ The team/company budget     │ and cheaper than the SaaS it  │
  │           │                             │ replaced                      │
  └───────────┴─────────────────────────────┴───────────────────────────────┘

  Recommendation: the customer is the team, sold bottom-up through the builder.
  One builder swipes a card. When the third builder at the same company shows
  up, you convert to a team plan — that's the Figma/Vercel motion and it's the
  only one that works here, because no CIO is going to buy "a cloud for small
  software" cold.

  The mistake to avoid: pricing hosting. Small software has ~zero infra cost and
  unlimited app count. Price builder seats, keep recipients free forever. Your
  unit of value is "a person who can ship internal tools," not a container.

  2. Who's the user

  This is the fork that decides your whole company. Pick one:

  - (a) Engineers building internal tools → you're fighting Vercel, Railway,
  Retool. They ship fine already. Weak wedge.
  - (b) Pure non-technical operators → biggest RFS prize, but they can't debug a
  failed build and your support cost eats you alive. Too early.
  - (c) The technical person on a non-technical team — the ops lead who writes
  SQL, the finance analyst with Claude Code, the founder-who-used-to-code. ←
  pick this

  (c) has real demand (their team asks for tools constantly), enough skill to
  survive an error message, and — critically — an audience already sitting next 
  to them. They're the bridge to (b): every recipient is a future builder once
  the tool is good enough.

  Then narrow further. Pick one vertical for the next 90 days where workflows
  are bespoke and no SaaS fits: recruiting agencies, clinics, PE/finance ops,
  logistics brokers, construction. Not "everyone with an idea." A vertical gives
  you templates that land on the first try and referrals that compound inside
  an industry.

  3. Why us

  Deploy is not the moat. Vercel does deploy. Be honest about that internally,
  or you'll build the wrong roadmap.

  The moat is the three things after deploy that nobody has bundled, and which
  are exactly the hard problems the RFS names:

  1. Identity and permissions with zero config. "Everyone @acme.com," "these 6
  people," roles, revoke on offboard. This is the #1 reason internal tools die
  on Vercel — the builder has to implement auth, gets it wrong, and IT kills it.
  You own this or you have no product.
  2. Safe execution of arbitrary code written by someone who isn't a security 
  engineer. Sandboxing, egress policy, secrets the app can read but the builder
  can't exfiltrate, an audit trail. This is what makes it sanctionable by the
  company — the thing that turns shadow IT into approved IT.
  3. A batteries-included environment the agent already knows. DB, storage,
  cron, email, internal API access as named primitives. Not because humans want
  convenience — because the agent needs a small, deterministic, self-describing 
  target. Every extra vendor is another chance for the agent to fail.

  Then the differentiator that's actually structural: your customer is an agent,
  not a human. packages/mcp and packages/skills are your real product surface.
  Deploy errors should be written to be fixed by an LLM, not read by a person.
  AWS will never do this — their whole surface is built for humans reading docs.
  That's your right to win: the first cloud whose API contract is optimized for
  a machine caller and whose sharing model is optimized for a non-technical
  recipient.

  Drop "iterate-to-green resident agent" as your headline. Every deploy platform
  will have that within a year. Lead with shared with your team in one click, 
  with auth and permissions handled.

  4. How to get people to use it

  In priority order:

  1. Be the deploy verb inside the agent. Your highest-leverage channel by far.
  Ship the MCP server + Claude Code skill + Cursor rules + AGENTS.md so that
  "deploy this and share it with my team" resolves to Supersonic automatically.
  You already have packages/skills — make that the #1 priority, not phase 5.
  Distribution happens inside the tool where the builder already is.

  2. Recipient-side pull. Every app's auth screen is your ad, seen weekly by 20
  colleagues who all have their own broken spreadsheet. Add one line: "Make your
  own." Recipients are free, so there's no friction — the loop is builder → 20
  recipients → 1 new builder. That's your compounding channel, and it only works
  if you nail #1 in "Why us" (recipients must not need to create an account).

  3. Templates as demand capture, per vertical. Not "todo app." The 20 tools
  every recruiting agency rebuilds: candidate pipeline, commission calculator,
  client status portal, interview scheduler. Each = a prompt + a deploy button.
  This is your SEO and your cold-outreach hook.

  4. Do the unscalable thing for 20 teams. Get on a call, build their first
  three tools live, hand them the keys, watch what breaks. Land the team, not
  the user. Your LinkedIn outreach tooling should be pointed at builders in one
  vertical, offering "I'll build your first tool on a call" — not "try our
  platform."

  Measure the right thing. Not deploys, not signups. Weekly active recipients 
  per app. If a builder ships 10 apps that nobody else opens, you built a toy.
  If they ship 2 apps that 15 colleagues open every Monday, you built the Google
  Docs of small software and the team plan sells itself.

  that wrote the code.

  Take that to 10 teams in one vertical. If they nod at "my colleagues can use
  it" and shrug at "one-click deploy," you've confirmed the pivot from your
  current README.

⏺ Rails is the right analogy, and it's sharper than "simpler." Rails didn't win
  on ease — it won on being opinionated. Convention over configuration: it
  assumed a shape of app and deleted every decision. That's the move.
  Vercel/Railway/Render sell you primitives to assemble. You'd sell an
  application — auth, DB, jobs, storage, domains, users are just there, one way,
  no choices. That's a different category from infra cloud, and it's defensible
  because incumbents can't remove options without breaking their existing
  customers.

  Three things I'd sharpen:

  1. Your real beneficiary of the conventions is the agent, not the human.
  This is the strongest version of your thesis and I don't think you're saying
  it yet. Rails conventions saved humans from decisions. Your conventions save
  the model from failure modes. Every choice you delete is a branch where the
  agent can hallucinate a wrong config. A cloud with one way to do things has a
  dramatically higher first-try success rate for agent-written code. That's
  measurable — "% of apps that deploy correctly on the first attempt,
  unattended" — and it's a benchmark you can beat Vercel on publicly. Nobody
  owns that number yet.

  2. Don't be downstream of Lovable — be horizontal to all of them.
  Lovable/Bolt/Replit are Heroku: their builder welded to their host. Your bet
  should be that the builder layer commoditizes (50 AI app builders, plus Claude
  Code, plus whatever's next) while the runtime layer wants to be shared and
  horizontal. Rails didn't ship an IDE. So: your target is reachable from any of
  them. And the near-term wedge is real — every Lovable app that needs a cron,
  a private DB, a real domain with SSO, or a call to an internal API has to
  eject, and there's nowhere good to eject to. Take the ejection, but don't
  build your identity around being the second stop.

  3. The population you're betting on is right, but don't wait for it.
  "Non-engineers with agents" is genuinely coming and will be much larger than
  100x engineers. But today it's small. So ride the trend line without depending
  on it: the people who exist right now are semi-technical builders — ops
  leads, founders, analysts, designers — using Claude Code/Cursor and hitting a
  wall at deploy. Serve them now, and the same product gets a 50x larger market
  as agents go mainstream. Same product, no pivot.

  One risk worth naming: Rails won as a free standard, then Heroku monetized it.
  Categories get created by conventions people adopt, not products people buy.
  Consider making the convention itself public — an open spec for "what a small
  app looks like" that any agent can target — and sell the cloud that runs it
  best. Otherwise a competitor adopts your conventions and out-executes on
  infra.

  Category line to try: the application cloud — you don't configure
  infrastructure, you just have an app.

  Deploy/redeploy speed — right but weakly differentiating. Vercel and Railway
  already nail this. Also, hackathon deploy frequency is actually low: you code
  for 20 hours and deploy at hour 22. The real pain is the last 30 minutes —
  "works locally, broken in prod, judges walking over." So the feature isn't
  speed, it's deploys that can't strand you: per-commit preview URLs, instant
  rollback, and no cold start when a judge opens the link. Reframe from fast to
  unbreakable.

  Web UI editing — this is the one I'd challenge. The desire is real, but
  building a visual editor makes you Lovable/v0/Onlook with a much smaller team,
  and it contradicts the horizontal-runtime bet you just made. That's how infra
  companies lose two years.

  There is a defensible version, and it's narrow: not a canvas — a prompt box 
  attached to the live app. "Make this filterable" → resident agent edits the
  repo → redeploys. You already have the deploy agent. Nobody owns "edit prod
  from prod," and only a cloud can build it, because it needs the running app +
  the repo + the agent in the same place. Lovable can't, because they don't own
  your repo after ejection. Ship that, not a style panel.

  Analytics — you're underrating this one. "Who exactly opened it" is the
  sleeper feature on your whole list. Umami and PostHog give you anonymous
  sessions. You own auth, so you can say rakhat@acme.com opened this 4 times 
  today with zero instrumentation, on by default. That falls out of your
  architecture and no analytics vendor can replicate it without you. It's also
  the exact number that proves the Google-Docs thesis.

⏺ Right instinct, wrong analogy — and the difference matters.

  What's genuinely right: Paper wins because a generation showed up with
  different expectations while Figma's surface calcified around its existing
  users. That structural asymmetry is real for you too: Vercel and Render are
  shaped by 2016 assumptions (git-centric, primitive-centric,
  dashboard-centric), and they can't remove options without breaking paying
  customers. You can. Also, nobody has made deploying feel good — infra is all
  control panels. Taste is genuinely available in this category, which is
  unusual.

  Where Apple/Android breaks down, though:

  1. Apple's opinionation rests on lock-in you don't have. Silicon, OS, store,
  switching costs. "We're the Apple" in a young infra company usually becomes
  permission to skip the unglamorous reliability work — and infra is judged on
  reliability first. Paper can ship a rough edge and lose nothing. A cloud that
  drops someone's app loses that customer forever. Taste earns the first deploy;
  uptime earns the second.

  2. Bad opinions cost differently. In a design tool, a wrong opinion is "mildly
  annoying, I'll work around it." In a cloud, it's "my app can't do what the
  business needs, now I migrate everything." So you need an escape hatch — eject
  to real infra without a rewrite. Heroku died partly because outgrowing it
  meant leaving angry. Build the graduation path on day one and it becomes a
  selling point: "you're never trapped." That's the opposite of Apple, and it's
  the version that actually works.

  Better comps: Linear vs Jira, or Heroku vs AWS. Opinionated, taste-forward,
  new-generation — but the opinions exist to remove failure modes, not to look
  nicer. Linear earned trust by being fast and correct, then the taste
  compounded.

  Three things I'd actually steal from Paper:

  - The sequencing. Don't convert Vercel power users. Target people whose first 
  deploy ever is on you. Habits calcify, and that cohort has none. That's your
  beachhead and it's the one incumbents structurally can't defend.
  - The vocabulary. Part of Paper's power is that it refuses Figma's words. If
  your UI says "environment variables," "container," "egress," you've already
  lost the new generation. Invent your own nouns. This is also how your
  conventions become the thing agents learn — same corpus point as before.
  - No settings page, not a settings page with good defaults. Very different
  products.

  One reframe: Apple vs Android implies same category, different taste. You said
  you want your own category — so don't position against Vercel at all. Paper
  isn't "the Apple of Figma," it's arguably a different tool for a different
