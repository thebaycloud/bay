Freeze here stops the simulated deploy where it is and leaves the film waiting on it, forever. That is the case to watch: the picture has to stay alive with no progress at all to show.

Four complaints, and what each one turned out to be

It was static

The worst of the four, and the one the other three hid behind. A stage played at ten times life — a thirty-three-second build went past in three — and then stopped dead while the deploy took another three minutes over it. So most of every deploy was a frozen frame, and the part that moved was too fast to read.

Both halves were one mistake: treating a beat's scripted duration as the speed to play it at. Now the film creeps. It opens a stage at a readable pace and then eases along a power law — still visibly moving at four minutes — approaching the end and never arriving, because the last of a stage belongs to the deploy. An asymptote is the only kind of progress bar that cannot lie.

And the picture stopped being wired to that clock. There are two clocks now: story time, paced by the deploy, and wall time, which never stops. Everything that is merely alive — the water, the welders, the gantry, the ferries, the traffic, the lamps — runs on the wall clock. The yard works whether or not the build does.

The camera sat still, then cut too fast

A shot used to be a move parameterised by story time, so it finished in three seconds and then held for three minutes. A shot is now a move on the wall clock that keeps drifting when the move is done — and it has a length. When the length runs out and the stage has not, the film cuts to another angle on the same subject. A long stage is an edited sequence, which is what a long stage is in any film.

The alternates are derived from the master rather than written: same target, a yaw, a height and a distance off it. Which is only safe because of the next one.

The camera went through things

There was no collision pass at all. There is one now, and it asks the right question: not how thick is this object but how much of the frame would it take up. A girder at arm's length is in the way; the same girder across the bay is the background. When the master framing is blocked the operator steps round and up until it is not — the same eight moves a real one would try.

The night was empty and the world had edges

Night was lit by ambient alone, because the key light followed a sun that is below the horizon for two thirds of the film. There is a moon now: it keys the scene, it lays a path on the water, and it gives the sky something to be about. Around it, a city whose windows are six banks on six different flickers, street light in the streets, five vessels crossing the bay under running lights, aircraft warning lights on the towers, an aeroplane, festoon lighting down the walls of the dock, and light pools on the quay under every lamp.

The world's edges went two ways. Every terrain now drowns at its own rim — the land bends under the sea before the model runs out, so the far distance is water going into haze rather than a cliff with sky behind it — and there is a ring of hills at the edge of sight that opens out to nothing over the ocean, which is the one direction with no land in it. The yard was the other half: it used to be concrete boxes on a patch of seabed, a raft moored in open water. It is cut into the hill now, with the dock cut out of the shelf and a channel dredged from the gates to deep water.

And there are people in it now — six of them, walking the apron, standing at the dock edge, kneeling at the seam with a welding arc. The motion study left itself that note and never acted on it: nothing in the picture was at human scale, so nothing said how big she is. They are also the cheapest movement in the film.
